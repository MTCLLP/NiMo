Reaching for a coffee mug on a high shelf causes a sharp pinch. Sleeping on your favourite side wakes you with a throbbing ache. Trying to comb your hair, put on a jacket, or throw a ball feels suddenly impossible.

Shoulder pain is the **third most common musculoskeletal complaint** seen in clinical medicine, accounting for over 16% of all orthopaedic consultations. [1] Up to **70% of people will experience a debilitating episode of shoulder pain** at some point in their lives. [2]

Yet, many patients spend months in a frustrating trial-and-error cycle—taking painkillers that merely mask symptoms, resting until the joint stiffens, or undergoing generic physiotherapy exercises that inadvertently aggravate the problem.

Why is the shoulder so prone to stubborn discomfort? Because **the shoulder sacrifices bony stability to give you the greatest range of motion of any joint in the human body.** [3]

In this clinical guide, we will unpack the functional anatomy of the shoulder, explore the six most common causes of shoulder pain, review self-check red flags, and detail the modern diagnostic and treatment pathways that guide you from pain to full functional recovery.

---

## Anatomy: A Golf Ball on a Tee

To understand what goes wrong, we must understand the unique mechanics of the **glenohumeral joint** (the main ball-and-socket shoulder joint). [3]

Unlike your hip joint—where a deep bony socket firmly encases the ball—the shoulder's socket (the glenoid cavity) is remarkably shallow and flat.

```text
       THE SHOULDER JOINT ARCHITECTURE
                      │
     ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
     ▼                                 ▼
[The Bony Anatomy]             [Dynamic Muscular Sleeve]
• Humeral Head (Large Ball)    • Rotator Cuff (4 Tendons)
• Glenoid Fossa (Shallow Cup)  • Supraspinatus (Top/Lift)
• Glenoid Labrum (Cartilage)   • Infraspinatus (Back/Turn)
  Deepens socket by 50%        • Teres Minor (Back/Turn)
                               • Subscapularis (Front/In)
```

The relationship is frequently compared to **a golf ball resting on a shallow tee**. [4] 

Because the bones provide minimal mechanical stability, the joint relies almost entirely on surrounding soft tissues:
1. **The Glenoid Labrum:** A rubbery fibrocartilaginous ring that rims the socket, deepening it by roughly 50% and creating a suction seal for the ball. [4]
2. **The Joint Capsule & Ligaments:** A strong, fibrous sleeve enclosing the articulation.
3. **The Rotator Cuff:** A dynamic sleeve of four tendons (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) that tightly grip the ball, actively compressing it into the centre of the socket whenever you lift, push, or rotate your arm. [5]

If any component of this delicate system fails—whether a tendon frays, the labrum tears, or the capsule contracts—the entire mechanical balance collapses, leading to friction, inflammation, and pain.

## The 6 Most Common Causes of Shoulder Pain

Because multiple structures sit within millimeters of each other, diagnosing shoulder pain requires pinpoint accuracy. The six primary culprits include:

```text
                     PRIMARY SHOULDER PAIN GENERATORS
                                    │
       ┌────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                            ▼                            ▼
[Rotator Cuff Pathology]     [Adhesive Capsulitis]        [Joint Instability]
• Tendinopathy / Bursitis    • "Frozen Shoulder"          • Labral (SLAP / Bankart)
• Calcific Tendinitis        • Capsular Fibrosis          • Subluxation / Dislocation
• Partial / Full Tears
       │                            │                            │
       ▼                            ▼                            ▼
[Impingement Syndrome]       [Arthritis (AC / GH)]        [Referred Pain]
• Subacromial Bone Spur      • Acromioclavicular Joint    • Cervical Radiculopathy
• Scapular Dyskinesis        • Glenohumeral Cartilage     • Diaphragm / Visceral
```

### 1. Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy & Subacromial Impingement
The most common cause of shoulder pain in adults over 35. [6] When you lift your arm, the rotator cuff tendons (principally the supraspinatus) must slide smoothly through a narrow subacromial space beneath the bony acromion roof. 

If an acromial bone spur develops, or if the rotator cuff is weak and inflamed, the tendon and its overlying lubricating bursa are painfully pinched against the bone—a condition known as **subacromial impingement** or **subacromial bursitis**. [6]

### 2. Rotator Cuff Tears
Rotator cuff tears range from partial-thickness fraying on the undersurface of the tendon to complete full-thickness retractions off the bone. [7]
- **Traumatic Tears:** Sudden tearing caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand, a heavy jerk while lifting, or a sports collision (common in younger athletes).
- **Degenerative Tears:** Gradual, age-related attrition where years of micro-friction and diminished microvascular blood supply cause the tendon to pull away from its insertion (common after age 50). [8]

For an in-depth breakdown, read our guide on [how do I know if I tore my rotator cuff?](/blog/how-do-i-know-if-i-tore-my-rotator-cuff)

### 3. Adhesive Capsulitis ("Frozen Shoulder")
Frozen shoulder is a unique condition characterized by severe, progressive inflammation and subsequent contracture (fibrosis) of the joint capsule. [9] The capsule thickens, shrinks, and adheres to the bone, mechanically locking the ball inside the socket.

Frozen shoulder classically progresses through **three distinct clinical stages**: [9]

```text
STAGE 1: FREEZING (2–9 Months)   ► Intense, diffuse pain; night pain; progressive stiffness
STAGE 2: FROZEN (4–12 Months)    ► Pain gradually subsides; profound stiffness; zero external rotation
STAGE 3: THAWING (12–24+ Months) ► Range of motion slowly returns over 1 to 2 years
```

Frozen shoulder is significantly more prevalent in individuals with diabetes mellitus (affecting up to 20% of diabetic patients), thyroid disorders, or following prolonged post-surgical shoulder immobilization. [10]

### 4. Shoulder Instability & Labral Tears (SLAP & Bankart)
Common in athletes under 35 and individuals involved in collision or overhead sports (cricket, swimming, tennis, volleyball). [11]
- **Bankart Lesion:** When the shoulder dislocates forward, the anterior-inferior labrum is stripped off the glenoid rim. Without anatomical repair, recurrent dislocations frequently follow. [12]
- **SLAP Tear (Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior):** The top part of the labrum, where the long head of the biceps tendon anchors, peels away due to repetitive overhead throwing or heavy traction. [13]

Review our detailed article on [shoulder dislocation causes and recovery](/blog/shoulder-dislocation-causes-and-recovery).

### 5. Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint Arthritis
The AC joint is the small articulation at the very top of your shoulder where your collarbone (clavicle) meets your shoulder blade (acromion). Unlike deep rotator cuff pain, AC joint pathology produces point-tenderness directly on top of the shoulder bony bump. It is particularly aggravated by cross-body reaches (reaching for a seatbelt) or heavy bench pressing ("weightlifter's shoulder"). [14]

### 6. Cervical Spine Radiculopathy (Referred Pain)
Not all shoulder pain begins in the shoulder. A pinched cervical nerve root in your neck (such as a herniated disc at C5–C6) can refer sharp, shooting pain into the top and outer side of the shoulder, often accompanied by numbness, tingling, or "pins-and-needles" down the arm into the fingers. [15]

## Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Medical Evaluation

While most shoulder conditions develop gradually, certain symptoms demand urgent orthopaedic assessment: [16]

- **Sudden Inability to Lift the Arm:** An acute drop after a fall or heavy jerk often indicates a massive, acute traumatic rotator cuff tear.
- **Severe Night Pain:** Unrelenting, deep aching that prevents sleep regardless of body position.
- **Visible Deformity or Prominent Step-Off:** Visible displacement of the collarbone or squaring-off of the shoulder contour indicates acute dislocation or severe AC joint separation.
- **Systemic Symptoms:** Shoulder pain accompanied by fever, chills, local skin redness, or heat suggests a rare but medical emergency: septic arthritis of the shoulder joint. [16]
- **Chest Pain / Left Shoulder Tightness:** Any acute left shoulder or jaw tightness accompanied by shortness of breath, dizziness, or diaphoresis must be evaluated immediately in an emergency department to rule out acute myocardial infarction.

## Clinical Diagnostic Roadmap

An accurate diagnosis relies on clinical expertise combined with multimodal imaging: [17]

```text
                  CLINICAL DIAGNOSTIC ROADMAP
                               │
       ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
       ▼                       ▼                       ▼
[Physical Examination Battery] [Plain Radiographs]   [High-Resolution MRI / US]
• Active vs passive ROM        • True AP & Axillary  • 3T MRI without radiation
• Neer & Hawkins impingement   • Acromial spurs      • Rotator cuff retraction
• Empty Can (Supraspinatus)    • Calcific deposits   • Labral tears (SLAP/Bankart)
• External rotation strength   • Glenohumeral space  • Dynamic ultrasound
• O'Brien & Apprehension tests
```

### 1. Active vs. Passive Range of Motion (The Critical Test)
- If you cannot lift your arm actively, but the doctor can lift it fully and painlessly for you, the problem is typically a **muscle/tendon failure** (such as a full-thickness rotator cuff tear or nerve palsy).
- If your arm is mechanically locked and neither you nor the doctor can move it past a certain angle (especially in external rotation), the problem is **capsular contracture** (frozen shoulder) or advanced arthritis. [9, 17]

### 2. Specific Provocative Tests
- **Hawkins-Kennedy & Neer Tests:** Passively elevating and internally rotating the flexed arm compresses the supraspinatus under the acromial arch, reproducing impingement pain. [18]
- **Empty Can (Jobe's) Test:** Testing resisted arm abduction in 90 degrees of elevation and 30 degrees of horizontal adduction with thumbs pointing down isolates supraspinatus strength and tears. [18]
- **Apprehension & Relocation Tests:** Abducting and externally rotating the arm to 90 degrees reproduces a sense of instability or impending dislocation in an anteriorly unstable shoulder. [12]

### 3. Imaging Modalities
- **Digital X-Rays (True AP, Scapular Y, Axillary views):** Essential first step to identify bony acromial spurs (Bigliani types I–III), calcific tendinitis, osteoarthritis, and subtle bone loss. [19]
- **3T High-Field MRI:** The gold standard for soft-tissue visualization. It defines the exact size, retraction grade, and muscle fatty atrophy of rotator cuff tears, as well as labral detachments. [20]
- **High-Resolution Dynamic Ultrasound:** Highly accurate for evaluating rotator cuff integrity and subacromial bursal impingement in real time during active movement. [21]

## The Step-by-Step Treatment Ladder

Treatment begins with the least invasive, biologically sound options, advancing to surgical intervention only when clearly warranted:

```text
                      THE TREATMENT LADDER
                               │
    LEVEL 1 ► Relative Rest, Load Management & Ergonomics
    LEVEL 2 ► Evidence-Based Physical Therapy & Scapular Re-education
    LEVEL 3 ► Image-Guided Therapeutic Injections (PRP / Corticosteroid)
    LEVEL 4 ► Minimally Invasive Shoulder Arthroscopy
```

---

## Level 1 & 2: Conservative Therapy & Scapular Re-Education

Over **70% to 80% of patients with shoulder impingement, bursitis, and small partial rotator cuff tears recover fully without surgery.** [22]

However, recovery requires active neuromuscular conditioning rather than passive resting.

### 1. The Scapular Foundation (Scapulothoracic Rhythm)
For every 3 degrees you raise your arm, roughly 2 degrees occurs at the shoulder joint and 1 degree occurs at the shoulder blade (scapula). [23]

If your scapular stabilizer muscles (serratus anterior, lower trapezius) are weak, your shoulder blade fails to upwardly rotate and tilt backward as you reach up. This causes the acromion to tip downward, pinching the rotator cuff like a pair of pliers. [23]

```text
             WEAK SERRATUS ANTERIOR & LOWER TRAPEZIUS
                                │
                                ▼
         Impaired Scapular Upward Rotation & Posterior Tilt
                                │
                                ▼
         Narrowed Subacromial Space During Arm Elevation
                                │
                                ▼
          Chronic Mechanical Pinching of Supraspinatus
```

Rehabilitation focuses heavily on:
- **Serratus Anterior Activation:** Scapular push-ups plus, wall slides with a resistance band.
- **Lower Trapezius Strengthening:** Prone Y-raises and horizontal rows.
- **Posterior Shoulder Stretching:** Addressing posterior capsule tightness via sleeper stretches and cross-body adduction.

## Level 3: Targeted Image-Guided Injections

When intense pain prevents active participation in physical therapy, targeted injections offer powerful relief: [24]

- **Subacromial Corticosteroid Injection:** Delivers rapid reduction of bursal swelling and acute synovitis. Always administered under ultrasound guidance to ensure the medication enters the bursa while strictly avoiding direct intratendinous injection (which can weaken collagen fibres). [25]
- **Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP):** Prepared from your own blood, PRP delivers concentrated growth factors (PDGF, TGF-beta) to stimulate cellular proliferation and tissue healing in chronic, degenerated rotator cuff tendinopathy. Clinical trials show PRP offers longer-lasting relief than steroids in active patients. [26]
- **Hydrodilatation (Hydrodistension) for Frozen Shoulder:** Injected under pressure into the contracted joint capsule, a sterile mixture of saline, local anaesthetic, and corticosteroid physically expands and stretches the fibrosed capsule from within, dramatically restoring passive range of motion. [27]

## Level 4: Minimally Invasive Shoulder Arthroscopy

When conservative management fails to relieve symptoms after a structured 3 to 6-month trial, or when an active patient sustains an acute full-thickness rotator cuff tear, minimally invasive **shoulder arthroscopy** is the definitive standard of care. [28]

```text
                    SHOULDER ARTHROSCOPY PORTALS
                                 │
     ┌───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┐
     ▼                           ▼                           ▼
[Subacromial Decompression] [Rotator Cuff Repair]     [Labral / Bankart Repair]
Shaving bony spurs to        Refixing retracted tendon  Anchoring torn labrum
widen subacromial space      with bioabsorbable anchors to restore joint stability
```

Performed through 3 or 4 tiny incisions (each 5 mm to 8 mm), a pencil-thin camera and specialized instruments address the specific structural pathology:

1. **Arthroscopic Subacromial Decompression (Acromioplasty):** Using a high-speed motorized burr, the surgeon shaves away the impinging acromial bone spur and removes the chronically inflamed bursa, creating a smooth, spacious ceiling for the rotator cuff to glide without friction. [29]
2. **Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair:** The torn tendon edge is mobilized, refreshed, and tightly re-anchored back to its original anatomical footprint on the humerus using high-strength suture anchors. Modern double-row and transosseous-equivalent techniques maximize tendon-to-bone contact area for robust biological healing. [30]
3. **Arthroscopic Labral & Bankart Repair:** In unstable shoulders, tiny suture anchors are placed along the glenoid rim to securely re-attach the torn labrum and tighten the stretched capsule, eliminating recurrent dislocations. [12]
4. **Biceps Tenodesis / Tenotomy:** If the long head of the biceps tendon is frayed or dislocated within the bicipital groove, re-anchoring it lower down the arm relieves anterior shoulder pain while preserving arm aesthetics and elbow flexion strength. [31]

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### Can a torn rotator cuff heal on its own without surgery?
A partial-thickness rotator cuff tear can become completely asymptomatic with dedicated physiotherapy, strengthening of the surrounding muscles, and load management. However, a complete full-thickness rotator cuff tear does not biologically re-attach to the bone on its own, because the constant elastic pull of the muscle continually retracts the torn tendon edge. In active individuals, timely arthroscopic repair prevents the tear from expanding and muscle tissue from turning into irreversible fat (fatty degeneration). [7, 8]

### Why does my shoulder hurt so much more at night when I lie down?
Night pain is a classic symptom of rotator cuff tendinopathy, bursitis, and frozen shoulder. When standing, gravity pulls the arm downward, naturally widening the subacromial space. When you lie down flat, that downward gravitational pull disappears, and the humeral head drifts upward, directly compressing the inflamed bursa and tendons against the acromial bone. Sleeping propped up on two pillows or placing a small pillow beneath your elbow significantly reduces this nocturnal compression. [6]

### How do I tell the difference between frozen shoulder and a rotator cuff tear?
The definitive differentiator is **passive range of motion**. In a rotator cuff tear, you cannot lift your arm on your own (active motion is limited), but another person or your doctor can easily lift your arm through a full range of motion (passive motion is preserved). In frozen shoulder, the joint capsule is physically shrunken and locked: neither you nor the doctor can move the arm, particularly when attempting to rotate the forearm outward (external rotation). [9, 17]

### Is it safe to continue working out at the gym with shoulder pain?
You should temporarily discontinue exercises that place your shoulder into provocative end-range overhead or behind-the-neck positions (such as military overhead barbell presses, behind-the-neck pulldowns, upright barbell rows, and deep dips). Substitute these with neutral-grip dumbbell presses, floor presses, face pulls, and seated rows kept within a pain-free range of motion. [22]

## Summary & Key Takeaways

- **A Balancing Act:** The shoulder's extreme mobility makes it inherently vulnerable to soft-tissue impingement, labral tears, and capsular contracture.
- **Accurate Diagnosis First:** Never settle for generic treatments. Differentiate between rotator cuff pathology, frozen shoulder, instability, and cervical referred pain using physical tests and 3T MRI imaging.
- **Conservative Care Is Highly Effective:** Most impingement and partial cuff tears resolve with structured scapular re-education, rotator cuff strengthening, and load management.
- **Timely Arthroscopic Repair:** For full-thickness tears in active patients or chronic refractory impingement, minimally invasive shoulder arthroscopy delivers excellent, durable pain relief and restores full athletic function.
