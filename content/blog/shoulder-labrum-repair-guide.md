The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the human body, capable of throwing a ball at 140 km/h, performing complex gymnastic manoeuvres, and rotating through an expansive 360-degree arc. However, this unmatched mobility comes with an inherent anatomical compromise: **instability**. [1]

The shoulder joint (glenohumeral joint) is structured essentially like a golf ball resting on a shallow tee. The spherical head of the humerus is three to four times larger than the flat glenoid socket of the shoulder blade. [1, 2] To keep this ball securely centred during dynamic motion, the body relies on a specialised ring of tough, rubbery fibrocartilage called the **glenoid labrum**. [2]

When the labrum is torn, whether from an acute shoulder dislocation, a hard fall, or repetitive overhead throwing, the joint loses its stabilising bumper and suction-cup seal. This leads to persistent pain, deep clicking, mechanical catching, or recurrent feelings that the shoulder is slipping out of its socket. [1, 3]

In this clinical guide, Dr Nihar Modi explains the **different types of shoulder labral tears**, how specialists diagnose them, how modern **arthroscopic keyhole repair** restores stability, and what recovery entails.

---

## What Is the Glenoid Labrum and Why Is It Critical?

The glenoid labrum performs three vital mechanical functions in shoulder stability: [2, 4]

1. **Deepening the Socket:** It effectively increases the functional depth of the glenoid cavity by up to 50%, providing a physical cartilaginous "chock-block" that prevents the humeral head from sliding off the edge.
2. **The Negative Intra-Articular Pressure Seal:** The labrum creates an airtight fluid seal with the joint capsule, maintaining negative atmospheric pressure that acts like a biological suction cup holding the ball into the socket. [4]
3. **Anchor Point for Essential Ligaments:** The labrum serves as the attachment site for the glenohumeral ligaments (the primary static stabilisers of the joint) and, superiorly, the long head of the biceps tendon. [2, 5]

When this fibrocartilaginous ring tears away from the bone, the humeral head translates excessively across the socket, causing tissue impingement, joint subluxation, and accelerated wear of the surrounding articular cartilage. [1, 3]

## The 3 Main Types of Shoulder Labral Tears

Labral tears are categorised primarily by **where on the glenoid rim** the damage occurs (often described using the face of a clock): [5, 6]

::labral-tear-clock
::

| Tear Type                                   | Anatomical Clock Location | Typical Mechanism of Injury                                                                                                        | Core Symptoms                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bankart Lesion (Anterior-Inferior)**      | 3:00 to 6:00              | Traumatic anterior [shoulder dislocation](/procedures/shoulder-dislocation-treatment) or subluxation (arm forced up and backward). | Sensation of the joint slipping out; apprehension when cocking the arm to throw. |
| **SLAP Tear (Superior Labrum Ant-to-Post)** | 10:00 to 2:00             | Repetitive overhead throwing, pulling/traction injuries, or falling onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH).                             | Deep anterior clicking; painful loss of throwing velocity ("dead arm" syndrome). |
| **Posterior Labral Tear (Reverse Bankart)** | 6:00 to 9:00              | Repetitive pushing loads (heavy bench press, football blocking) or posterior impact.                                               | Deep, dull posterior shoulder ache; clicking during push-ups or weightlifting.   |

### 1. Bankart Lesions (Anterior Instability)

A **Bankart lesion** occurs when the humeral head dislocates out of the front of the joint, peeling the anterior-inferior labrum and the attached inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL) directly off the glenoid bone. [3, 7] In active patients under 30, a traumatic dislocation without surgical stabilisation carries a recurrence rate exceeding **70% to 80%**, as the unhealed labral tissue leaves the anterior door of the joint permanently loose. [7, 8] Bankart tears are frequently accompanied by a **Hill-Sachs lesion** (an impaction compression fracture on the posterior humeral head). [7]

### 2. SLAP Tears (Superior Labrum Anterior-to-Posterior)

The superior labrum anchors the long head of the biceps tendon. During high-velocity throwing, the violent "peel-back" torsional forces generated during the late cocking and early deceleration phases can twist and tear the labrum off the top of the glenoid. [5, 9] SLAP tears are classified into four primary types based on the Snyder classification: [5]

- **Type I:** Fraying of the superior labrum with an intact biceps anchor (treated conservatively or with simple debridement).
- **Type II (Most Common):** Complete detachment of the superior labrum and biceps anchor from the superior glenoid tubercle (requires anatomical anchor refixation or biceps tenodesis). [9]
- **Type III:** Bucket-handle tear of the superior labrum with an intact biceps anchor.
- **Type IV:** Bucket-handle tear extending upward directly into the biceps tendon itself.

### 3. Posterior Labral Tears

Less frequent than anterior tears, posterior labral injuries are prevalent among collision athletes (rugby, kabaddi, football) and heavy strength-training enthusiasts who perform high-volume horizontal pressing. [6, 10] Symptoms often present as a deep posterior ache during bench presses or dips rather than dramatic dislocation.

## Symptoms: How to Know If You Have a Labral Tear

Because the labrum is buried deep within the joint capsule, labral tears rarely cause visible external swelling. Instead, patients typically experience mechanical and functional warning signs: [3, 11]

- **Mechanical Catching & Clicking:** A sharp, audible pop or catching sensation during arm rotation or overhead reaches.
- **Pain with Specific Arm Positions:** Discomfort when lifting the arm overhead, reaching across the chest, or reaching into the back seat of a car.
- **"Dead Arm" Syndrome in Overhead Athletes:** Inability to generate normal velocity or accuracy when serving in tennis, spiking in volleyball, or bowling/throwing in cricket. [9]
- **Apprehension & Instability:** An unsettling sense that your arm is "sliding out of place" or loose when your arm is cocked back.
- **Night Pain:** A deep, persistent ache when lying directly on the affected shoulder.

## Diagnostic Evaluation: Tests and Imaging

An accurate diagnosis requires combining dynamic physical examination manoeuvres with modern cross-sectional imaging: [11, 12]

### 1. Specialised Physical Examination Tests

- **O'Brien Active Compression Test (for SLAP Tears):** The patient extends the arm forward at 90 degrees with the thumb pointing downward (internal rotation) against downward resistance, and the test is repeated with the palm turned upward. Deep joint pain that is present with the thumb down but relieved with the palm up strongly indicates a SLAP lesion. [13]
- **Anterior Apprehension and Relocation Test (for Bankart Tears):** While lying flat, the examiner gently moves the arm into abduction and external rotation. If the patient feels apprehension or fear of dislocation, the examiner applies stabilising posterior pressure on the humeral head. Immediate relief of apprehension confirms anterior capsulolabral instability. [7, 11]
- **Crank Test & Kim Test:** Dynamic axial loading manoeuvres designed to elicit clicking or pain as the torn labrum catches between articular surfaces.

### 2. Advanced Imaging: Why 3T MRI or MR Arthrography Is Essential

A standard X-ray does not visualise soft cartilage; its primary role is to assess glenoid bone loss or detect a Hill-Sachs fracture. [8]  
To visualise the labrum directly, a **high-resolution 3.0-Tesla MRI** or an **MR Arthrography (MRA)**, where a tiny volume of dilute gadolinium contrast dye is injected into the shoulder joint under ultrasound guidance, is the gold standard. [12] The contrast fluid seeps directly into the tear gap, creating a crisp white outline that distinguishes true tears from normal age-related anatomical variants (such as a sublabral foramen or Buford complex). [12]

## How Is Shoulder Labrum Repair Performed?

Modern labral stabilisation is performed almost exclusively via **minimally invasive [shoulder arthroscopy](/procedures/shoulder-arthroscopy)**: [2, 14]

::labral-repair-steps
::

1. **Minimally Invasive Portals:** Two to three tiny 4 mm keyhole incisions are made around the shoulder. A 4K pencil-sized arthroscope illuminates the joint, while specialised micro-instruments are introduced through clear working cannulas.
2. **Labral Mobilisation:** The scarred, retracted labrum is gently freed from the glenoid neck until it can be repositioned anatomically without excessive tension. [14]
3. **Decortication of the Glenoid Rim:** A motorised micro-burr lightly rasps the cortical surface of the glenoid bone to expose rich vascular cancellous bone (creating a biological "bleeding bone bed" essential for cartilage-to-bone healing). [2, 14]
4. **Biocomposite Knotless Suture Anchors:** Tiny 1.8 mm to 2.9 mm biocomposite or all-suture anchors which gradually absorb or incorporate into native bone are drilled into the glenoid edge. High-strength orthopaedic sutures are passed around the labrum, tensioning the capsule and firmly locking the labral bumper back against bone. [14, 15]

In select older overhead patients or recreational athletes with complex Type II SLAP tears, **biceps tenodesis** (re-anchoring the long head of the biceps tendon further down the humerus, outside the joint) is increasingly preferred over superior labral repair, as extensive multicenter trials show faster pain relief and lower postoperative stiffness. [9, 16]

## Staged Rehabilitation Timeline After Labrum Repair

Tendon and cartilage healing requires careful biological protection. Here is what to expect phase by phase: [17, 18]

| Recovery Phase               | Timeline       | Primary Objective                                      | Motion & Exercise Guidelines                                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Phase 1: Protection**      | Weeks 0 to 4   | Protect suture anchors; manage pain & swelling.        | **Sling worn 24/7.** Hand, wrist, and gentle pendulum exercises. External rotation limited to neutral for Bankart; zero active biceps flexion for SLAP. [17] |
| **Phase 2: Early Motion**    | Weeks 5 to 8   | Restore passive and active-assisted range of motion.   | Wean from sling: gentle pulley and cane exercises. Forward elevation progressed to 140°+. Scapular posture retraining.                                       |
| **Phase 3: Strengthening**   | Weeks 9 to 16  | Rebuild dynamic rotator cuff & periscapular stability. | Introduce light resistance bands. Internal/external rotation strengthening. Closed-kinetic-chain push-up plus and wall slides. [18]                          |
| **Phase 4: Return to Sport** | Months 4 to 6+ | Sport-specific agility, power, and contact clearance.  | Plyometric ball drops, interval throwing protocol, sport-specific agility drills. Clearance upon reaching 90%+ strength symmetry. [19]                       |

## Non-Surgical Treatment vs Surgery: When Is Repair Necessary?

Not all labral tears require surgery. An evidence-based orthopaedic specialist evaluates multiple patient-specific criteria before recommending an operation: [3, 8]

### Conservative Management Is Appropriate When:

- The tear is an isolated, non-displaced SLAP Type I or posterior fraying without joint instability.
- The patient is a recreational athlete willing to modify overhead loading.
- High-level physical therapy targeting **periscapular neuromuscular control** and dynamic rotator cuff stabilisation successfully eliminates pain and restores full function. [3, 17]

### Arthroscopic Surgical Repair Is Strongly Indicated When:

- **Recurrent Instability or Subluxation:** The shoulder repeatedly slips or pops out during daily movements or sports. [7, 8]
- **Young, Active Contact Athletes:** In patients under 25 who participate in collision or overhead sports, conservative management after a first traumatic dislocation carries an unacceptable recurrence rate of up to 80%. Early arthroscopic Bankart stabilisation reduces recurrence to **under 5% to 10%**. [7, 8]
- **Failure of 3 to 6 Months of Conservative Therapy:** Persistent mechanical locking, catching, and sleep-disrupting pain despite high-compliance rehabilitation. [1, 16]
- **Accompanying Bony Lesions:** Significant capsulolabral avulsion compromising joint containment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do suture anchors stay in my shoulder?

Modern labral surgery uses **biocomposite or all-suture anchors**. Biocomposite anchors (made from poly-L-lactic acid and osteoconductive beta-tricalcium phosphate) slowly bioabsorb over 2 to 4 years, gradually being replaced by natural host bone. All-suture anchors are tiny, flexible fibre sleeves that remain permanently nestled inertly within the bone socket without setting off metal detectors or interfering with future MRIs. [15]

### When can I drive after shoulder labral surgery?

Driving is generally safe once you have completely stopped prescription narcotic pain medications, no longer need to wear your sling during the day (typically between weeks 4 and 6), and have demonstrated sufficient dynamic arm control to perform sudden, emergency steering manoeuvres safely with both hands. [17]

### What is the success rate of arthroscopic Bankart repair?

In peer-reviewed clinical registries, arthroscopic Bankart repair achieves a successful stabilisation **rate of approximately 90% to 94%**, allowing over 85% of athletes to return to their pre-injury level of sport when bone loss is minimal (<13% to 15% glenoid bone loss). [7, 8, 19] If significant bone loss has occurred from multiple dislocations, a bone-augmentation procedure (such as a Latarjet coracoid transfer) may be recommended instead.
