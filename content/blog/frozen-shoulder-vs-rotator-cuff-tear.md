Shoulder pain is one of the most common musculoskeletal complaints evaluated in clinical orthopaedics, affecting up to 20% of adults at some point in their lives. [1] Among adults over the age of 40, two conditions account for the vast majority of severe, debilitating shoulder symptoms: **Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder)** and **Rotator Cuff Tears or Tendinopathy**. [1, 2]

To the patient, the symptoms can feel remarkably identical:

- A deep, dull toothache-like pain centered over the lateral shoulder.
- Sharp, agonising pain spikes when reaching behind your back to tuck in a shirt or fasten a bra.
- Inability to comfortably reach high cupboards or slip your arm into a jacket sleeve.
- Severe nocturnal pain that wakes you up the moment you roll onto the affected side. [1, 3]

Because of these overlapping symptoms, patients frequently misdiagnose themselves or are misdiagnosed, leading to delayed treatment or, even worse, the wrong therapy (such as aggressive stretching forced on an inflamed, freezing shoulder, or attempting strengthening exercises on a torn tendon). [2, 4]

Yet, anatomically and biologically, **these two conditions are fundamentally opposite**. In this clinical guide, Dr Nihar Modi explains how to differentiate frozen shoulder from a rotator cuff tear, the pivotal clinical tests used during examination, and the tailored treatment pathways for each.

---

## The Definitive Clinical Differentiator: Active vs. Passive Motion

If you remember only one clinical rule when distinguishing between these two conditions, remember this: **the difference lies in what happens when someone else moves your arm.** [2, 5]

::active-vs-passive-motion
::

### 1. In a Rotator Cuff Tear: A "Pulley & Motor" Problem

The rotator cuff is a muscular sleeve composed of four tendons (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) that dynamically lift and rotate the humeral head. [6] When a tendon is torn, the "cable" is broken. You cannot generate active muscular tension to lift your own arm against gravity. **However, the joint space itself is supple.** When an orthopaedic surgeon supports your elbow and lifts your arm (passive motion), your arm moves freely through its full normal arc overhead. [2, 5]

### 2. In a Frozen Shoulder: A "Contracted Biological Container" Problem

In adhesive capsulitis, the rotator cuff tendons are usually completely intact. Instead, the problem lies in the **joint capsule,** the tough, fibrous envelope surrounding the joint. [4] This capsule becomes severely inflamed, thickens, and shrinks tightly around the bone like a shrunken wool sweater. [3, 7] As a result, the joint is mechanically locked from the inside. **Neither you nor the examining physician can move the arm**, regardless of how relaxed your muscles are. [2, 5]

## Condition 1: Understanding Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis)

In a healthy shoulder, the joint capsule is loose and pliable, with an internal fluid capacity of **15 to 20 mL**, allowing the humeral head to glide freely. [3] In adhesive capsulitis, an abnormal inflammatory cascade causes fibroblasts to lay down dense, contracted Type I and Type III collagen, shrinking the capsule's volume down to a tight **3 to 5 mL**. [4, 7] The coracohumeral ligament and the rotator interval become fibrotic bands that physically block the arm from turning outward. [7]

### The 3 Classical Clinical Stages

Frozen shoulder follows a predictable tripartite clinical timeline (first documented by Codman and Reeves): [3, 8]

| Stage                                 | Duration       | Primary Clinical Feature                                           | Patient Experience & Management Focus                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stage 1: Freezing (Painful Stage)** | 2 to 9 Months  | Severe, unremitting inflammation; progressive loss of motion.      | Intense ache at rest and severe night pain. **Avoid aggressive stretching**; focus on gentle motion and anti-inflammatory control. [4, 8] |
| **Stage 2: Frozen (Stiff Stage)**     | 4 to 12 Months | Active inflammation subsides; profound mechanical stiffness peaks. | Pain decreases at rest, but the arm is rigidly stiff. Daily tasks like grooming or dressing are severely restricted. [8]                  |
| **Stage 3: Thawing (Recovery Stage)** | 6 to 24 Months | Biological remodelling; capsular pliability slowly returns.        | Range of motion steadily improves; structured physical therapy is most effective here. [8, 9]                                             |

### Key Risk Factors

Why does frozen shoulder occur? While primary adhesive capsulitis can arise spontaneously (idiopathic), it has powerful systemic correlations: [3, 10]

- **Diabetes Mellitus:** Patients with diabetes have a **five-fold higher risk** (up to 20% lifetime prevalence) of developing frozen shoulder, often experiencing more severe contractures and bilateral involvement due to excessive collagen glycosylation. [10]
- **Thyroid Disorders:** Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism significantly elevate risk. [10]
- **Post-Traumatic / Post-Surgical Immobilisation:** Keeping an arm in a sling too long after a wrist fracture, breast surgery, or cardiac procedure.
- **Gender & Age:** Approximately 70% of cases occur in women between the ages of 40 and 65. [3]

## Condition 2: Understanding Rotator Cuff Pathology

Unlike the global contracture of frozen shoulder, rotator cuff disorders represent a spectrum of localised tendon injury: [6, 11]

::tendon-tear-spectrum
::

- **Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy / Bursitis:** Micro-tears and inflammatory thickening within the tendon, frequently aggravated by subacromial spurring (impingement). [1, 6]
- **Partial-Thickness Tears:** The tear penetrates either the articular (undersurface) or bursal (top surface) fibres without completely snapping through.
- **Full-Thickness Tears:** The tendon has completely detached from its footprint on the greater tuberosity of the humerus. [11]

### Common Symptoms of Rotator Cuff Tears

- **Painful Arc of Motion:** Pain is sharpest when the arm is raised between **60 and 120 degrees** (as the inflamed tendon squeezes under the acromion bone), but subsides once the arm is elevated fully overhead. [1, 12]
- **True Muscular Weakness:** Inability to hold the arm elevated against resistance.
- **Audible Crepitus:** A rough grating or clicking sensation as the ragged tendon edge glides beneath the acromial arch. [6]

## The Physical Examination: 3 Tests Your Specialist Performs

During your clinical consultation, your orthopaedic surgeon uses targeted physical examination manoeuvres to pinpoint the true culprit: [5, 12]

### 1. The Passive External Rotation Test (The Gold Standard)

With you seated and your elbows bent at 90 degrees, tucked snugly against your ribs, the surgeon gently rotates both forearms outward:

- **In Frozen Shoulder:** The affected forearm cannot turn outward (often stopping at 0° to 20°, compared to 60° to 80° on your normal side). It hits an abrupt, rigid mechanical "end-feel." [2, 5]
- **In a rotator cuff tear,** the forearm rotates outward smoothly and symmetrically with minimal resistance.

### 2. The "Drop Arm" Sign (for Full-Thickness Supraspinatus Tears)

The examiner passively raises your arm fully overhead to 180 degrees and asks you to lower it down slowly to your side:

- **Positive Test:** As the arm lowers to about 90 degrees, the supraspinatus cannot support the arm against gravity, causing the limb to drop suddenly and uncontrollably to your side. [12] This strongly indicates a massive rotator cuff tear.

### 3. The Neer & Hawkins Impingement Tests

Passive forward flexion and internal rotation manoeuvres that compress the rotator cuff tendon against the coracoacromial arch. These elicit sharp pain in rotator cuff impingement, whereas frozen shoulder pain occurs at the absolute terminal limit of all motion planes. [1, 12]

## Diagnostic Imaging: What Do Scans Reveal?

An evidence-based diagnostic protocol relies on sequential imaging: [13, 16]

1. **Standard Shoulder Radiographs (X-Rays):**
   - In both conditions, plain X-rays of the glenohumeral joint appear largely normal. Their essential clinical value is **ruling out other masquerading conditions**: severe glenohumeral osteoarthritis (bone-on-bone joint loss), calcific tendinitis (hydroxyapatite deposits), or occult bone tumours. [1]
2. **High-Resolution 3.0T MRI:**
   - **For Frozen Shoulder:** MRI reveals characteristic thickening of the coracohumeral ligament (>4 mm) and posteroinferior capsular oedema in the axillary recess, while confirming that the rotator cuff tendons are structurally intact. [13]
   - **For Rotator Cuff Tears:** MRI clearly delineates tear size (small, medium, large, massive), tear retraction (Patte classification), tendon edge quality, and whether muscle atrophy or fatty infiltration (Goutallier stage) has occurred. [11, 16]

## Treatment Pathways: Why Precision Matters

Applying the wrong treatment can significantly prolong your recovery. Here is how modern, evidence-based management differs:

::shoulder-treatment-pathways
::

### Treatment for Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis)

1. **Never Force Violent Stretching in Stage 1:** During the inflammatory "freezing" phase, aggressive stretching triggers severe reactive synovitis, accelerating capsular contracture and worsening pain. [4, 8]
2. **Ultrasound-Guided Intra-Articular Corticosteroid Injection:** Administering a targeted steroid injection directly into the glenohumeral joint during the first 6 to 12 weeks drastically curbs synovial inflammation, shortens the painful freezing phase, and facilitates early mobility. [14, 17]
3. **Hydrodilatation (Capsular Distension):** Under real-time ultrasound guidance, a high volume of sterile saline and local anaesthetic is slowly injected into the joint capsule until high pressure stretches or ruptures the tight capsular adhesions from within. Multiple randomised trials demonstrate immediate improvements in range of motion. [18]
4. **Arthroscopic Capsular Release:** If profound mechanical stiffness persists beyond 6 to 9 months despite conservative care, a 30-minute keyhole arthroscopic procedure can be performed. The surgeon uses an electrocautery radiofrequency probe to divide the tight rotator interval and release the contracted inferior axillary pouch, restoring full range of motion immediately. [7, 19]

### Treatment for Rotator Cuff Pathology

1. **Kinetic Chain Physical Therapy:** Restoring periscapular balance, serratus anterior activation, and thoracic spine mobility to offload the subacromial space. [15]
2. **Biological Therapies:** Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) injections can promote cellular repair in partial-thickness tendinopathies that have failed physical therapy. [20]
3. **Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair:** For full-thickness tears with progressive weakness or acute traumatic tears in active individuals, arthroscopic suture anchor fixation re-anchors the tendon to bone before irreversible muscle retraction occurs. [6, 11]

## Can You Have Both at the Same Time?

**Yes.** In clinical practice, this is known as **Secondary Adhesive Capsulitis**. [3, 7]

When a patient suffers a painful rotator cuff tear, the natural subconscious protective response is to guard the arm and avoid moving the shoulder. If the arm is held motionless for weeks or months, the inflamed, stagnant joint capsule begins to contract and fibrose secondarily.

When both conditions coexist, the treating orthopaedic surgeon must address the stiff joint capsule first: attempting to surgically repair a rotator cuff tendon in a severely stiff, frozen joint leads to unacceptable rates of permanent postoperative stiffness. [7, 19]

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does frozen shoulder always heal on its own?

While textbook literature often labels frozen shoulder as "self-limiting," long-term clinical outcome studies (such as those by Hand et al.) reveal that up to **40% of patients continue to experience residual stiffness, end-range pain, or functional restrictions beyond three years** if left untreated. [3, 8] Timely intervention with anti-inflammatory injections and staged therapy dramatically shortens this duration.

### Will an MRI definitively tell me which condition I have?

Yes. A high-resolution 3.0T MRI evaluated by an experienced musculoskeletal radiologist and orthopaedic specialist provides near 95% sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing full-thickness rotator cuff tears from the capsular thickening characteristic of frozen shoulder. [13, 16]

### Can aggressive massage fix a frozen shoulder?

No. Deep tissue massage of the surrounding trapezius and neck muscles can relieve secondary muscular tension, but it cannot stretch the deep, internal glenohumeral joint capsule. Forcing the arm through rigid contractures can tear muscle fibres or provoke severe joint inflammation. [4, 8]
