A sharp, pinching pain at the front of your hip when standing up from a low chair. A dull, deep ache in the groin after a morning run, football match, or gym squat session. An uncomfortable restriction when tying your shoelaces or getting into a car.

These everyday frustrations are classic hallmarks of **Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI)**, commonly known as **hip impingement**. [1]

Once thought to be rare, FAI is now recognised by sports medicine specialists and orthopaedic surgeons worldwide as one of the primary mechanical drivers of hip pain, labral tears, and early hip osteoarthritis in active individuals aged 18 to 50. [2]

Receiving a diagnosis of hip impingement often leaves patients with urgent questions: *Do I need surgery right away? Can physical therapy fix a bony bump? What are the chances of returning to competitive sport or pain-free daily living?*

In this comprehensive guide, we review the full spectrum of evidence-based treatment options for FAI—from modern non-operative rehabilitation protocols and ultrasound-guided injections to joint-preserving hip arthroscopy.

---

## What Happens Inside an Impinging Hip?

To understand how treatments work, we must first look at the biomechanics of the hip joint. The hip is a ball-and-socket joint where the rounded femoral head (the ball) fits snugly inside the acetabulum (the pelvic socket). [3]

In an anatomically ideal hip, the ball glides smoothly inside the socket through wide arcs of flexion and rotation. In an impinging hip, subtle bony overgrowths create premature friction:

```text
NORMAL HIP                       CAM IMPINGEMENT                 PINCER IMPINGEMENT
  ┌────────┐                       ┌────────┐                     ┌──────────┐
  │ Socket │                       │ Socket │                     │ Over-cup │ ◄── Deep/Over-covered
  └───┬────┘                       └───┬────┘                     └───┬──────┘     Socket Rim
      │                                │                              │
   ( Ball )                         ( Ball )                       ( Ball )
      │                                │ ◄── Bony "Cam" Bump          │
      │                                │     at Neck Junction         │
  [Femur Neck]                     [Aspherical Neck]              [Femur Neck]
```

- **Cam Morphology:** An extra ridge or "bump" of bone forms on the neck of the thigh bone. As the hip flexes, this aspherical bump wedges into the socket rim, peeling the articular cartilage off the bone. [4]
- **Pincer Morphology:** The rim of the pelvic socket extends too far, over-covering the ball and crushing the protective fibrocartilaginous labrum against the neck. [4]
- **Combined (Mixed) Impingement:** Over 70% of symptomatic patients possess features of both cam and pincer morphology. [5]

Crucially, **having cam or pincer bone morphology on an X-ray does not automatically mean you require invasive treatment.** According to the international **Warwick Agreement on FAI Syndrome**, a clinical diagnosis requires a triad of three factors: characteristic symptoms, positive physical examination signs, and confirmatory radiological imaging. [6]

If you have bony bumps on imaging without pain or mechanical catching, no treatment is needed. Treatment is exclusively indicated when impingement produces symptoms, functional restriction, and progressive tissue irritation.

## Treatment Decision Tree: Where Do You Start?

Modern hip preservation follows a graduated, stepwise approach. We categorise treatment into three primary tiers:

| Tier | Treatment Modality | Primary Objectives | Typical Timeline |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Tier 1** | Activity Modulation & Specialist Physiotherapy | Restore lumbopelvic control, strengthen hip abductors/extensors, avoid impingement arcs | 6 to 12 weeks |
| **Tier 2** | Diagnostic & Therapeutic Joint Injections | Confirm pain origin, suppress acute synovitis, enable progressive rehabilitation | Single procedure with 4–12 week window |
| **Tier 3** | Minimally Invasive Hip Arthroscopy | Reshape bone (osteochondroplasty), repair/re-anchor labrum, restore joint fluid seal | 4 to 9 months recovery to sport |

## Tier 1: Conservative Management & Physical Therapy

For the majority of patients with newly diagnosed FAI, the first line of care is non-operative management. [6]

It is biologically true that exercises cannot "melt away" an extra bony bump. However, **abnormal hip biomechanics and muscle imbalances heavily amplify the frequency and severity of bony collision.** [7]

```text
       Weak Gluteus Medius & Deep Hip Rotators
                         │
                         ▼
           Anterior Femoral Head Glide
                         │
                         ▼
   Premature Pinching of Labrum Against Acetabular Rim
                         │
                         ▼
        Recurrent Synovial Inflammation & Groin Pain
```

Targeted rehabilitation aims to optimise how the ball centers within the socket during movement, preventing unnecessary end-range collision.

### 1. Temporary Activity & Range-of-Motion Modulation
The initial phase focuses on calming irritated synovial tissue and avoiding positions that provoke impingement:
- **Avoid Forced Deep Squatting:** Replace below-parallel squats with box squats or split squats kept within a comfortable hip flexion angle (<90 degrees). [8]
- **Ergonomic Sitting:** Elevate your office chair or car seat so your hips sit higher than your knees. Avoid sitting with your legs crossed, which drives the hip into provocative flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. [8]
- **Cross-Training Adjustments:** If long-distance running or breaststroke swimming provokes groin pain, substitute with upright stationary cycling (with a high saddle) or deep-water pool running.

### 2. Neuromuscular Hip & Pelvic Stabilization
High-level evidence, including the landmark **UK FASHIoN Trial** and **Australian FAIT Trial**, underscores that non-surgical programmes must be structured and progressive rather than generic stretching. [9, 10]

Effective FAI rehabilitation emphasizes:
- **Gluteal Muscle Recruitment:** Strengthening the gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, and deep hip external rotators (such as the gemelli and obturator muscles) maintains dynamic femoral head centralization. [7]
- **Avoiding Anterior Hip Flexor Dominance:** Patients with FAI frequently over-activate their psoas and tensor fasciae latae (TFL) to compensate for sluggish gluteals, driving the ball forward into the socket.
- **Lumbopelvic Core Control:** Retraining the transverse abdominis and pelvic floor prevents excessive anterior pelvic tilt, which functionally deepens the socket and worsens pincer contact during walking and running. [11]

## Tier 2: Diagnostic and Therapeutic Injections

When persistent hip inflammation prevents a patient from engaging in rehabilitation, or when symptoms overlap with lower back or adductor issues, targeted injections serve as an invaluable clinical tool. [12]

Because the hip is a deep joint enclosed by thick neurovascular structures, **hip injections should always be performed under precise ultrasound or fluoroscopic (X-ray) guidance.** [13]

```text
                    ULTRASOUND PROBE
                           │
       ┌───────────────────┴───────────────────┐
       ▼                                       ▼
 [Accurate Needle Placement]       [Avoidance of Femoral Nerve & Artery]
       │
       ▼
 [Direct Delivery into Joint Capsule]
```

### 1. Local Anaesthetic (Diagnostic Hip Block)
A fast-acting anaesthetic (such as ropivacaine or lidocaine) is placed directly into the intra-articular hip space. If the patient experiences immediate, substantial pain relief (70%–90%) while performing previously painful manoeuvres (such as walking, pivoting, or squatting) over the following few hours, it confirms beyond doubt that the hip joint—rather than the lumbar spine or pubic symphysis—is the true pain generator. [14]

### 2. Corticosteroid Injections
A low-dose anti-inflammatory steroid provides powerful temporary reduction of secondary joint synovitis. This relief typically lasts between 6 and 12 weeks, creating a critical "therapeutic window" during which an athlete can rebuild gluteal strength without debilitating pain. [15] Repeated steroid injections are avoided, as multiple intra-articular steroid exposures can accelerate cartilage wear.

### 3. Hyaluronic Acid & Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP)
For active patients with mild secondary cartilage softening, autologous **Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP)** or viscosupplementation injections are increasingly utilised. While PRP does not remodel bony cam or pincer deformities, clinical trials indicate it can deliver anti-inflammatory cytokines, support synovial lubrication, and provide longer-lasting symptomatic relief than steroids without damaging cartilage tissue. [16]

## When Is Surgery Indicated for Hip Impingement?

While conservative therapy successfully manages a significant subset of individuals, surgery is considered when clear criteria are met: [6, 17]

1. **Persistent Symptoms:** Groin pain, catching, or stiffness persisting after a structured 3 to 6-month trial of specialist hip rehabilitation.
2. **Definitive Mechanical Conflict:** Radiographs, 3D CT scans, or 3T MRI confirming clear cam asphericity (alpha angle >55–60 degrees) or pincer over-coverage with corresponding labral or chondral damage. [18]
3. **Preserved Joint Space:** The joint space on plain weight-bearing radiographs remains well preserved (>2 mm to 3 mm). Hip arthroscopy is a **joint-preservation** procedure designed to protect healthy cartilage; it is not indicated for advanced, bone-on-bone hip osteoarthritis. [19]
4. **Mechanical Symptoms:** Persistent clicking, locking, or sharp catching that physically disrupts athletic participation or routine occupational demands.

## Tier 3: Minimally Invasive Hip Arthroscopy

Hip arthroscopy is one of the most rapidly advancing fields in modern orthopaedic surgery. Performed through 2 to 3 keyhole incisions (each roughly 1 cm in length), a high-definition camera (arthroscope) and specialised microscopic instruments are introduced into the hip joint under traction. [20]

```text
                        KEYHOLE PORTALS
                               │
       ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
       ▼                       ▼                       ▼
 [Cam Resection]       [Labral Refixation]     [Capsular Closure]
 (Shaving the bump)    (Anchoring the seal)    (Restoring stability)
```

Modern hip arthroscopy addresses both the primary cause (the bony deformity) and the secondary effect (the damaged labrum and cartilage):

### 1. Femoral Osteochondroplasty (Cam Resection)
Using a high-speed specialised burr under real-time fluoroscopic and arthroscopic visualisation, the surgeon precisely trims away the aspherical bone bump at the femoral head-neck junction. This restores the femoral neck's natural concavity, creating a spherical ball that glides cleanly through full internal rotation and flexion without colliding. [21]

### 2. Acetabuloplasty (Pincer Trimming)
If pincer morphology is present, the overhanging acetabular rim bone is gently recessed back to a normal anatomical angle, relieving excessive impingement stress on the joint rim. [21]

### 3. Labral Repair vs. Debridement
In the past, torn labral tissue was often simply shaved away (debridement). Extensive biomechanical and clinical studies have since proven that preserving the labrum is vital for long-term hip health. [22] 

Today, surgeons perform **labral refixation**: tiny bioabsorbable or knotless suture anchors are embedded into the acetabular bone, tightly securing the torn labral tissue back to the rim. This reconstitutes the airtight fluid seal, protecting articular cartilage from destructive shear stresses. [23]

### 4. T-Capsulotomy and Routine Capsular Closure
To navigate instruments inside the constrained hip joint, the surrounding capsule is carefully opened. At the completion of the procedure, routinely suturing the hip capsule closed (capsular plication/closure) is critical. Failure to close the capsule can lead to subtle micro-instability and prolonged postoperative aching. [24]

## Conservative vs. Surgical Outcomes: What Does Science Say?

In recent years, landmark multicentre randomised controlled trials have provided definitive clarity on the outcomes of FAI treatments:

- **The UK FASHIoN Trial (Lancet 2018):** Comparing hip arthroscopy to personalised hip therapy (PHT) across 23 hospitals, patients in both groups demonstrated clinical improvement. However, the hip arthroscopy group achieved **statistically and clinically superior hip-related quality of life outcomes (iHOT-33 scores)** at 12-month follow-up compared to physiotherapy alone. [9]
- **Return to Sport Meta-Analyses:** Large systematic reviews examining competitive and recreational athletes undergoing hip arthroscopy report an overall **return-to-sport rate of 85% to 92%**, with roughly 80% returning to their pre-injury level of performance within 6 to 9 months post-surgery. [25]

```text
• Ideal for mild impingement / low demands  • Corrects underlying bony conflict
• Zero surgical recovery downtime           • Protects labral seal & cartilage
```

## Postoperative Rehabilitation Roadmap

Surgery creates the anatomical environment for success; your post-operative rehabilitation determines the functional outcome. Recovery proceeds through structured phases: [26]

### Phase 1: Joint Protection & Crutch Weaning (Weeks 0 to 4)
- **Weight-Bearing:** Partial flat-foot weight-bearing on crutches for 2 to 3 weeks to protect the repaired labrum and healing bone.
- **Motion Restoration:** Passive circumduction and gentle stationary cycling without resistance starting within 48 hours to prevent intra-articular adhesions.
- **Avoidance:** Active straight leg raises and deep hip flexion past 90 degrees are restricted during early tissue healing.

### Phase 2: Gait Normalization & Core Stability (Weeks 4 to 8)
- Discontinuation of crutches once a normal, non-limping walking pattern is achieved.
- Progressive activation of the gluteus medius, gluteus maximus, and deep rotator complexes.
- Non-impact cardiovascular conditioning on an elliptical trainer or pool swimming (flutter kick only).

### Phase 3: Dynamic Strength & Linear Running (Weeks 8 to 16)
- Progressive resistance training: Romanian deadlifts, step-ups, goblet squats, and lateral band walks.
- Introduction of an interval-based, graduated return-to-running programme on an even, shock-absorbing surface once limb symmetry tests exceed 80%. [27]

### Phase 4: Sport-Specific Agility & Plyometrics (Months 4 to 6+)
- Deceleration drills, lateral cutting, multi-directional agility ladder work, and jumping/landing mechanics.
- Clearance for unrestricted sport upon meeting objective performance, strength, and psychological readiness criteria.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### Can hip impingement resolve on its own without any treatment?
The physical bony variation (the cam or pincer bump) will not disappear on its own. However, an acute flare-up of inflammation can settle with rest, activity modification, and rehabilitation. Many active individuals successfully manage their hip impingement non-surgically by avoiding repetitive end-range collision and keeping their kinetic chain strong. [6]

### Will hip arthroscopy prevent me from needing a hip replacement later in life?
The primary goal of modern hip preservation surgery is to restore normal joint biomechanics, repair the labral seal, and prevent progressive damage to the articular cartilage. Long-term studies indicate that patients who undergo arthroscopy before significant cartilage loss occurs have a substantially lower rate of conversion to total hip replacement compared to untreated symptomatic hips. [28]

### What happens if I ignore hip impingement pain?
Continuing high-impact, deep-flexion sports through sharp hip pain can turn an isolated labral tear into extensive articular cartilage delamination (chondral flap tears). Once cartilage is permanently lost, the joint transitions into early-stage osteoarthritis, at which point minimally invasive arthroscopy becomes less effective. [2, 19]

### How do I know if my pain is coming from my hip joint or a groin strain?
Hip joint pain classically presents deep in the anterior groin crease, often producing a "C-sign" where patients cup their thumb and index finger around the outside and front of the hip. In contrast, adductor muscle strains are tender to direct touch along the inner thigh muscle tendon. A specialised clinical evaluation and targeted MRI can confirm the exact source.

## Summary & Key Takeaways

- **A Triad of Factors:** Hip impingement is a clinical diagnosis requiring symptoms, physical signs, and imaging confirmation—not an X-ray report alone.
- **Tiered Approach:** Start with structured physiotherapy focused on gluteal strengthening, anterior pelvic control, and load management before considering invasive options.
- **Injections Offer Clarity:** Ultrasound-guided diagnostic injections confirm whether the hip joint is the genuine source of discomfort and can calm acute synovitis.
- **Timely Intervention Matters:** For patients with ongoing mechanical symptoms and failed conservative care, hip arthroscopy reliably reshapes bony contours, refixes the labrum, and enables high rates of return to sports and daily active life.
